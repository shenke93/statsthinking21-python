Statistical thinking: 
- Use simple terms (capture essential aspects).
- With uncertainty but try to describe the world accurately.
  - Human intuition often goes wrong, an example is [how do Americans perceive crime in their country](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/04/24/what-the-data-says-about-crime-in-the-us/).

Three major things we can do with statistics:
- **Describe**, from complex to simplified.
- **Decide**, in the face of uncertainty.
- **Predict**, based on knowledge of the previousness.

Example: Is saturated fat in our diet a bad thing?
- Human intuition/common sense: bad.
- Authority figure, longstanding dogma: bad.
- Actual scientific research, the PURE study: not bad.
  - Describe: 135000 people from 18 different countries, followed for a median of 7.4 years.
    - Result: 
      - People who ate more saturated fat were less likely to die, the lowest death rate seen for people who were in the fourth quintile s(who ate more fat than the lowest 60% but less than the top 20%). 
      - The opposite is seen for carbohydrates: the more carbs a person ate, the more likely they were to die. 
  - Decide: the relationships in the data occur randomly or not?
  - Predict: generalize from the data we have to some other situation, often in the future, i.e. used by a life insurance company

The big ideas of statistics:
- Learning from data: hypotheses, prior knowledge
- Aggregation: "the science of throwing away data"
- Uncertainty: statistical analysis can never "prove" a hypotheses, in the sense of demonstrating that it must be true (as one would in a logical or mathematical proof)
- Sampling from a population: representativenss and sample size
- Causality: "correlation does not imply causation, but it sure is a hint"; causation can be demonstrated using studies that experimentally control and manipulate a specific factor, in medicine such a study is referred to as a randomized controlled trial (RCT).
